Where you incorporate looks like a formation task. It behaves like an operating decision, because changing it later costs time, tax and approvals. The reverse-flip debate in India shows that cost in public.

## What a flip and a reverse flip are

A flip puts an offshore holding company, often in the US or Singapore, above an Indian operating company. A reverse flip undoes it, so investors and founders hold shares directly in the Indian company, as [The American Bazaar describes it](https://americanbazaaronline.com/2026/02/20/indian-startups-pause-reverse-flip-plans-and-rethink-moving-back-475570/). Outlook Business names the chance to list on Indian stock exchanges as the main reason companies reverse flip, and it [reported in January 2025](https://www.outlookbusiness.com/start-up/zeptos-homecoming-gets-nclt-nod-how-indian-start-ups-reverse-flipping-concept-work) that Zepto received approval from India's National Company Law Tribunal for its reverse flip from Singapore.

## The pause

In February 2026 The American Bazaar reported that Indian startups were pausing reverse-flip plans, and it named PhonePe, Meesho, Razorpay, Pine Labs, Udaan and KreditBee, citing Business Standard. It listed moderating valuations, a sell-off in Indian IT stocks and tax uncertainty as the causes. Sanjay Khan Nagra, a partner at Khaitan & Co, said some companies "have put plans on hold due to costs, income considerations, and execution challenges."

I read the American Bazaar report and not the Business Standard piece it cites, and I could not find a reliable count of completed reverse flips in 2026. So I do not know the current pace. The lesson for founders does not depend on the count.

## What it teaches

Structure choices made to raise money get reopened later for other reasons. A company that put a US holding company above its Indian business to raise from US investors may weigh a listing in India years afterward. The reasons to change arrive on a schedule you did not set: a listing venue, a tax rule or a shift in valuations.

Five questions to ask at formation:

1. Where will most of your customers and revenue sit in five years?
2. Where will your next investors expect the cap table to sit?
3. Where will you list, and who might acquire you?
4. What would moving cost in tax, approvals and time? Ask counsel for a number now.
5. Who owns the compliance calendar in each country?

I am an operator, not a lawyer or a tax adviser. Take these questions to someone who is.

## What the operator sees

Entity setup is the first step in a sequence: entity, bank, tax registration, filings. Each step blocks the next, a point I make in the [field guide to cross-border operations](/cross-border-operations). At Neuralzome Cybernetics I handled entity setup, outbound-investment compliance and US banking, and I closed the company's first US customers. A structure that nobody can explain slows every later step, including diligence.

Keep a one-page structure memo: each entity, its owners, its jurisdiction, its filing dates and who files. Put it in the data room. I built data rooms and investor memos for two space-tech ventures, and a clear structure memo speeds up diligence when a company has more than one entity.

## For investors

Before a round, ask the founder for the structure memo and for counsel's estimate of what it would cost to unwind the structure. The answers show whether the founder has thought about the exit, and they cost the founder an afternoon.
