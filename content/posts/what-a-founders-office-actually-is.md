Most people hear "founder's office" and picture someone taking notes in board meetings. In practice, across four ventures and four countries, it's looked more like: setting up a US entity from a laptop in India, negotiating a hostel-style guesthouse deal in the Maldives, or building the spreadsheet that finally told a founder where their money was actually going.

The job title changes — Head of International Operations, Events & Experience Curator, Founder's Office Associate — but the function is the same: you are the person a founder can hand a half-formed idea to and trust that it comes back as something operational.

## The three things that never change

1. **You're closest to the founder's actual priorities**, not the org chart's version of them.
2. **You operate with minimal oversight**, especially in 0-to-1 environments where there's no playbook yet.
3. **You're expected to close the loop** — not just plan the GTM launch, but watch it through to the first customer.

That last one is the part people underestimate. Anyone can build a plan. Founders don't need more plans. They need someone who'll still be there when the plan meets a customs form, a visa delay, or a vendor who ghosts on delivery day.

## Why cross-border makes it harder — and more useful

Every one of my roles has involved at least two countries. That means compliance headaches (ODI filings, multi-currency billing, work-visa navigation) that most operators never touch. But it also means founders lean on you harder, because you're solving problems they can't see from where they're sitting.

If you're building a founder's office function into your own company, my one piece of advice: hire for judgment under ambiguity before you hire for domain expertise. The domain, you can teach. The judgment either survived a 0-to-1 environment already, or it hasn't been tested yet.
